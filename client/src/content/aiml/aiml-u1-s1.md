### 1. Evolution and Definition of Artificial Intelligence (AI)


### 1.1 Definition of Artificial Intelligence

Core Definition (John McCarthy, 1956): Artificial Intelligence is "the science and engineering of
making intelligent machines, especially intelligent computer programs" capable of performing
tasks that typically require human intelligence—such as perception, learning, reasoning, decision-
making, and language understanding.
- **Human vs. Artificial Intelligence:** Natural intelligence emerges biologically in human/animal
brains through evolution and experience; Artificial Intelligence replicates or approximates
cognitive abilities synthetically using algorithms, mathematical models, data, and digital hardware.
Russell & Norvig's Four Formal Perspectives of AI (Standard University Framework): Every
textbook definition of AI falls along two axes—Thought vs. Behavior (internal reasoning vs.
external action) and Human Performance vs. Rationality (doing what humans do vs. doing the
- **mathematically optimal thing):**

The Turing Test (Alan Turing, 1950 — "Imitation Game"):
- **Setup:** A human interrogator sits in one room and communicates via text terminal with two
hidden respondents—one human and one machine.
- **Pass Criterion:** If the interrogator cannot reliably tell which respondent is the machine after
sustained questioning, the machine exhibits human-level intelligent behavior.
- **Required Capabilities:** Passing requires Natural Language Processing (communication),
Knowledge Representation (storing facts), Automated Reasoning (answering questions), and
Machine Learning (adapting to new patterns).

### 1.2 Evolution and Historical Milestones of AI

1943 — First Artificial Neuron Model: Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts published the first
mathematical model of a biological neuron, laying the theoretical seed for neural networks.
1950 — Alan Turing's Landmark Paper: Published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence"
asking "Can machines think?" and proposing the Turing Test.
1956 — Birth of AI (Dartmouth Conference): John McCarthy (widely called the Father of AI),
Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon organized the Dartmouth Summer
Workshop, officially coining the term "Artificial Intelligence".
1956–1973 — Early Enthusiasm & Symbolic AI: Creation of the Logic Theorist (first AI program
proving math theorems), Arthur Samuel's self-learning Checkers program (coined "Machine
Learning", 1959), and Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA (1966, first chatbot).
- **1974–1980 — First AI Winter:** Severe funding cuts occurred because early computers lacked
memory/speed and rule-based systems failed on real-world combinatorial explosion.
- **1980–1987 — Boom of Expert Systems:** Commercial success of rule-based Expert Systems
such as MYCIN (blood infection diagnosis) and XCON (DEC computer configuration), followed by
the Second AI Winter (1987–1993) as hand-coded rule bases proved brittle and costly to
maintain.
1997–2011 — Statistical ML & Landmark Victories: Shift from hand-coded rules to data-driven
Machine Learning. IBM Deep Blue (1997) defeated World Chess Champion Garry Kasparov; IBM
Watson (2011) won Jeopardy! using NLP.
- **Unit-I:** Foundations of Artificial Intelligence • Fundamentals of AIML

2012–Present — Deep Learning & Generative AI Era: Driven by GPUs and Big Data, AlexNet
(2012) revolutionized Computer Vision; DeepMind AlphaGo (2016) defeated Go champion Lee
Sedol; the Transformer architecture (2017) enabled modern Large Language Models (LLMs)
and multimodal Generative AI.

### 2. Types of Artificial Intelligence

- **Dual Classification Standard:** In university curricula, Artificial Intelligence is systematically
classified along two independent axes: (1) Based on Capabilities (how broadly intelligent the
system is compared to humans) and (2) Based on Functionality (how the system processes
memory, awareness, and interaction).
- **Type-I:** Based on Capabilities (Scope)

### 1. Narrow AI (ANI / Weak AI) — Exists Today

Excels at ONE specific task (e.g., Siri, Spam Filter, Chess)

### 2. General AI (AGI / Strong AI) — Theoretical

Human-level reasoning & transfer across ANY cognitive task

### 3. Super AI (ASI) — Hypothetical Future

Surpasses collective human intellect in every domain
- **Type-II:** Based on Functionality (Behavior)

### 1. Reactive Machines [Present]

No memory; reacts purely to current state (IBM Deep Blue)

### 2. Limited Memory [Present — Most Modern AI]

Uses past data/short-term history (Self-Driving Cars, LLMs)

### 3. Theory of Mind [Research / Future]

Understands human emotions, beliefs, intentions socially

### 4. Self-Aware AI [Hypothetical]

Possesses genuine consciousness, self-awareness & sentience

### 2.2 Classification Based on Functionality


### 1. Reactive Machines (Stateless): The most basic AI type. They analyze only the present

situation and choose the best immediate action without storing memories or learning from past
episodes (e.g., IBM Deep Blue evaluating chess board states; basic Netflix collaborative filters).

- **### 2. Limited Memory:** Stores historical training data and recent real-time observations temporarily

to make informed predictions. Nearly all modern Machine Learning and Deep Learning
applications—including autonomous vehicles (tracking nearby car speeds/lanes) and chatbots
—belong here.

### 3. Theory of Mind (Psychological Understanding — Under Research): Future systems

capable of modeling human mental states—beliefs, desires, emotions, and social cues—to
interact empathetically as true social partners.

### 4. Self-Aware AI (Consciousness — Hypothetical): The theoretical pinnacle where machines

possess internal self-awareness, subjective feelings, and independent consciousness. Does not
exist today.

### 3.1 Natural Language Processing (NLP)

- **Definition:** Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the AI domain enabling computers to read,
understand, interpret, translate, and generate human natural languages (such as English or Hindi)
in text or spoken form.
- **Two Core Sub-Components:** 
NLU (Natural Language Understanding): Maps messy, ambiguous human text into
structured machine-readable meaning (handling synonyms, sarcasm, grammar, and context).
- **NLG (Natural Language Generation):** Converts structured internal data/decisions back into
fluent, grammatically correct human sentences.
- **Unit-I:** Foundations of Artificial Intelligence • Fundamentals of AIML

Five Standard Stages of the NLP Pipeline:

### 1. Lexical Analysis (Tokenization & Normalization): Splits raw text into words/tokens,

lowercases text, removes punctuation/stop words (the, is), and reduces words to root form via
Stemming or Lemmatization (e.g., "running" → "run").

### 2. Syntactic Analysis (Parsing & POS Tagging): Assigns Parts of Speech (Noun, Verb,

Adjective) and checks grammatical structure via a parse tree (rejects invalid grammar like
"School goes to boy").

- **### 3. Semantic Analysis:** Extracts literal dictionary/contextual meaning and verifies logical validity

(rejects grammatically valid nonsense like "Hot ice cream").

- **### 4. Discourse Integration:** Resolves meaning across adjacent sentences (e.g., resolving

- **pronouns:** in "Riya bought a laptop. It is fast.", "It" refers to "laptop").

- **### 5. Pragmatic Analysis:** Interprets real-world speaker intent beyond literal words (e.g.,

recognizing "Can you open the window?" as a polite request, not a yes/no ability question).
- **Worked NLP Trace Example:** Input: "The students are studying AI!" → Tokenize:
["The", "students", "are", "studying", "AI"] → Stop-word Removal: ["students",
- **"studying", "AI"] → Lemmatize:** ["student", "study", "AI"] → Intent/Entity:
Action=study, Subject=student, Topic=AI.
- **Applications:** Machine translation (Google Translate), spam filtering, sentiment analysis on
product reviews, text summarization, and chatbots.

### 3.2 Computer Vision (CV)

- **Definition:** Computer Vision (CV) is the AI domain that enables machines to acquire, process,
analyze, and extract meaningful semantic information from digital images and video streams—
effectively giving computers "eyes" and visual understanding.
- **How a Computer "Sees" an Image:** A digital image is stored strictly as a 2D or 3D numerical
- **matrix of pixels:** 
- **Grayscale Image:** 2D matrix of intensity values ranging from 0 (pure black) to 255 (pure
white).
- **Color (RGB) Image:** 3D matrix consisting of three stacked 2D channels—Red (R), Green (G),
and Blue (B)—each pixel represented as a triplet (R, G, B).
Five-Stage Computer Vision Pipeline:

- **### 1. Image Acquisition:** Capturing raw frames using cameras, medical scanners (MRI/X-ray),

drones, or LiDAR sensors.

- **### 2. Pre-processing:** Noise filtering, resizing, brightness/contrast normalization, and grayscale

conversion.

- **### 3. Feature Extraction:** Detecting edges, corners, textures, and shapes (traditionally via filters;

today automatically via Convolutional Neural Networks — CNNs).

### 4. Detection / Classification / Segmentation: Labeling what is in the image and precisely where

each object is located.
