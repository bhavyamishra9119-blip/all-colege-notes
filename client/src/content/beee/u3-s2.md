3. p–n Junction Diode

### 3.1 Basic Definition & Structure

- **Definition:** A two-terminal semiconductor device formed on a single continuous crystal (Si or Ge)
where one half is doped P-type and the adjoining half is doped N-type.
- **Single-Crystal Rule:** Mechanically pressing a separate P-block and N-block together will not
work because microscopic surface discontinuities block carrier flow; the junction must be grown
inside one unbroken crystal (by diffusion, alloying, or ion implantation).
- **Terminals:** 
- **Anode (A):** Metal contact connected to the P-region.
- **Cathode (K):** Metal contact connected to the N-region (marked by a vertical bar in the symbol
and a silver/colored band on a real diode body).
- **Unilateral Device:** Permits heavy current in one direction (Anode → Cathode) and blocks current
in the reverse direction.

### 3.2 Zero-Bias (Unbiased) Operation — Formation of Depletion Region


### 1. Step 1 — Concentration Difference: Right after fabrication, the P-side has a huge density of

holes while the N-side has a huge density of free electrons.

### 2. Step 2 — Diffusion Across Junction: Driven by this concentration gradient, holes diffuse from P

→ N and electrons diffuse from N → P (creating initial Diffusion Current).

### 3. Step 3 — Recombination Near Boundary: Crossing electrons and holes meet near the junction

and annihilate (recombine) each other.

### 4. Step 4 — Uncovered Immobile Ions:

When a hole leaves the P-side, it leaves behind a fixed, immobile negative acceptor ion
(NA−).
When an electron leaves the N-side, it leaves behind a fixed, immobile positive donor ion
(ND+).

### 5. Step 5 — Depletion Region (Space-Charge Region): A narrow zone (≈ 0.5 – 1 μm wide) forms

across the junction that is completely depleted of mobile carriers and contains only fixed − ions (P-
side) and + ions (N-side).
- **UNIT–3:** Semiconductor Devices & Applications and Transistors


### 6. Step 6 — Built-in Electric Field & Barrier Potential (VB):

The uncovered + ions (N-side) and − ions (P-side) create an internal electric field pointing from
N → P.
This internal voltage hill is called the Barrier Potential (or Contact Potential): VB ≈ 0.7 V for
Silicon and VB ≈ 0.3 V for Germanium at 300 K.

### 7. Step 7 — Dynamic Equilibrium: The barrier repels further majority diffusion until Diffusion

Current exactly cancels minority Drift Current → Net Diode Current = 0 A.

### 3.3 Biasing of a p–n Junction Diode


### 1. Forward Bias (Diode Conducts — ON State):

- **Connection:** External battery positive (+) → P-side (Anode); battery negative (−) → N-side
(Cathode).
- **Carrier Action:** Like charges repel — battery + pushes P-side holes toward the junction;
battery − pushes N-side electrons toward the junction.
Effect on Barrier & Depletion Width: Applied voltage VF opposes internal barrier VB → net
barrier drops to VB − VF → depletion layer becomes very narrow.
- **Current Flow:** Once VF > Vγ (0.7 V Si / 0.3 V Ge), the barrier collapses → massive majority-
carrier forward current (IF in mA) flows easily with very low resistance.

### 2. Reverse Bias (Diode Blocks — OFF State):

- **Connection:** External battery negative (−) → P-side (Anode); battery positive (+) → N-side
(Cathode).
- **Carrier Action:** Opposite charges attract — holes are pulled away from the junction toward −;
electrons are pulled away toward +.
Effect on Barrier & Depletion Width: Applied voltage VR reinforces VB → net barrier rises to
VB + VR → depletion layer becomes very wide.
- **UNIT–3:** Semiconductor Devices & Applications and Transistors

- **Current Flow:** Majority-carrier current becomes zero; only a tiny, thermally generated minority-
carrier leakage current called Reverse Saturation Current (I0) flows (nA in Si; μA in Ge).

### 4. V–I Characteristics of a p–n Junction Diode


### 4.1 Concept & Experimental Setup

- **Definition:** A graph plotting the current through a diode (ID on the vertical Y-axis) against the
voltage applied across it (VD on the horizontal X-axis).
- **Measurement Circuit Elements:** 
Variable DC supply to sweep applied voltage smoothly.
Series Current-Limiting Resistor (R): Essential to prevent excessive current from burning out
the diode in forward bias.
- **Ammeter in Series:** Milliammeter (mA) for forward bias; microammeter (μA) for reverse bias.
- **Voltmeter in Parallel:** Measures exact junction voltage VD.

### 4.2 Forward Characteristics (1st Quadrant: +VF vs. +IF)

Region A — Below Cut-in Voltage (0 < VF < Vγ):
External voltage is too weak to overcome the built-in potential barrier VB.
Forward current stays practically zero.
Cut-in / Knee / Threshold Voltage (Vγ):
The critical forward voltage where the potential barrier collapses and current suddenly shoots
upward.
- **Standard Values:** Vγ ≈ 0.7 V (Silicon) and Vγ ≈ 0.3 V (Germanium).
Region B — Above Cut-in Voltage (VF > Vγ):
Current rises exponentially for very small increases in VF.
The diode offers very low dynamic resistance (typically 5 – 25 Ω).
- **UNIT–3:** Semiconductor Devices & Applications and Transistors


### 4.3 Reverse Characteristics (3rd Quadrant: −VR vs. −IR)

Region A — Reverse Saturation Region (0 < VR < VBR):
A tiny, nearly flat leakage current called Reverse Saturation Current (I0 or IS) flows due to
thermally generated minority carriers.
- **Typical Magnitude:** Nanoamperes (nA) in Silicon (superior thermal stability); microamperes
(μA) in Germanium.
- **Temperature Rule:** I0 is independent of VR but approximately doubles for every 10 °C rise in
temperature.
Region B — Reverse Breakdown Region (VR ≥ VBR):
At a high reverse voltage called the Breakdown Voltage (VBR), reverse current increases
abruptly while reverse voltage stays nearly fixed.
Mechanism 1 — Avalanche Breakdown (VBR > 6 V, lightly doped junctions): High electric
field accelerates minority carriers to high velocity; they collide with atoms and knock out new
electrons in a cumulative chain reaction (impact ionization).
Mechanism 2 — Zener Breakdown (VBR < 6 V, heavily doped junctions): Ultra-narrow
depletion layer produces an intense electric field (> 106 V/cm) that directly tears valence
electrons out of covalent bonds.
- **Safety Note:** In an ordinary rectifier diode, entering breakdown without current limiting causes
overheating and permanent junction destruction.
