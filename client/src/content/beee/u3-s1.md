### 1. Band Theory of Solids


- **### 1.1 Starting From Scratch:** Atomic Energy Levels to Energy Bands

- **Step 1 — Single Isolated Atom:** 
An atom consists of a positively charged nucleus surrounded by negatively charged electrons
revolving in discrete shells (K, L, M, N).
Every shell corresponds to one sharp, fixed energy value; electrons cannot exist between two
shells.
The electrons in the outermost shell are called Valence Electrons — they decide all electrical
and chemical properties of the material.
Step 2 — Bringing Many Atoms Together (Solid Crystal):
A solid crystal packs approximately 1023 atoms per cubic centimeter at very small interatomic
spacing (≈ 2 – 5 Å).
The outermost valence electron clouds of neighboring atoms overlap and exert electrostatic
forces on one another.
- **UNIT–3:** Semiconductor Devices & Applications and Transistors

Step 3 — Pauli’s Exclusion Principle:
In any interacting quantum system, no two electrons can occupy the exact same energy
state (identical set of quantum numbers).
Therefore, when N identical atoms merge into a crystal, each sharp atomic energy level splits
into N slightly different sub-levels.
- **Step 4 — Formation of Energy Bands:** 
Because N is huge (≈ 1023), the spacing between adjacent sub-levels is microscopic
(≈ 10−23 eV).
These tightly packed sub-levels merge into a virtually continuous range of allowed energies
called an Energy Band.
Inner-shell electrons are tightly shielded near the nucleus and barely split; outer valence levels
experience the strongest splitting.

### 1.2 Essential Terms in an Energy Band Diagram


- **### 1. Valence Band (VB):** 

The highest energy band occupied by valence electrons at absolute zero (0 K).
Electrons in the VB are tightly bound inside chemical bonds between parent atoms.
They cannot move freely through the crystal to produce electric current.
Its upper energy edge is labeled EV.

- **### 2. Conduction Band (CB):** 

The lowest unfilled or partially filled energy band lying directly above the valence band.
Once an electron absorbs enough energy to enter the CB, it breaks free from its parent atom
and becomes a free electron (conduction electron).
Free electrons accelerate easily under an applied electric field, constituting electric current.
Its lower energy edge is labeled EC.

- **### 3. Forbidden Energy Gap (Eg):** 

The energy separation between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction
- **band:** Eg = EC − EV.
No allowed electron energy states exist inside this gap; an electron must jump completely
across Eg in one step to conduct.
- **UNIT–3:** Semiconductor Devices & Applications and Transistors

Measured in electron-volts (eV), where 1 eV = 1.6 × 10−19 J (the energy gained by one
electron moving across 1 Volt).

- **### 4. Fermi Energy Level (EF):** 

At absolute zero (0 K), EF is the highest energy level occupied by electrons.
At any temperature T > 0 K, EF is the reference energy level where the probability of finding an
electron is exactly 50% (0.5).

### 1.3 Classification of Solids Based on Band Theory

- **Category 1 — Conductors (Metals:** Copper, Aluminum, Silver, Gold):
- **Band Structure:** Valence band and conduction band overlap (Eg = 0 eV), or the conduction
band is already partially filled.
- **Carrier Availability:** Huge pool of free electrons (≈ 1022 cm−3) exists even at 0 K; even a tiny
battery voltage drives a large current.
- **Temperature Behavior (PTC):** Conductors have a Positive Temperature Coefficient of
resistance — heating makes metal atoms vibrate harder, causing more electron collisions,
which increases resistance.
Category 2 — Semiconductors (Silicon, Germanium, Gallium Arsenide):
- **Band Structure:** Possess a small forbidden energy gap (Eg ≈ 1.12 eV for Si; Eg ≈ 0.67 eV for
Ge at 300 K).
- **At Absolute Zero (0 K):** Zero thermal energy → VB is 100% full, CB is 100% empty → acts as
a perfect insulator.
- **At Room Temperature (300 K):** Thermal heat supplies enough energy for some valence
electrons to jump into the CB → produces moderate conductivity.
- **Temperature Behavior (NTC):** Semiconductors have a Negative Temperature Coefficient of
resistance — heating breaks more covalent bonds, multiplying free charge carriers and
decreasing resistance.
Category 3 — Insulators (Diamond, Glass, Quartz, Rubber, Mica):
- **Band Structure:** Possess a very wide forbidden energy gap (Eg > 3 eV; typically 5 eV to
15 eV; Diamond is 5.5 eV).
- **Carrier Availability:** Room-temperature thermal energy (kT ≈ 0.026 eV) cannot push valence
electrons across such a huge barrier.
- **Result:** CB remains completely empty → practically zero conduction until extreme electric
field causes physical dielectric breakdown.

### 2. Intrinsic and Extrinsic Semiconductors


### 2.1 Atomic & Crystal Foundation (Silicon & Germanium)

- **Group-IV Tetravalent Elements:** 
- **Silicon (Si):** Atomic number 14 → electronic configuration 2, 8, 4 (4 valence electrons).
- **Germanium (Ge):** Atomic number 32 → electronic configuration 2, 8, 18, 4 (4 valence
electrons).
- **Covalent Bonding in the Crystal:** 
Every atom seeks a stable outer shell of 8 electrons (octet rule).
Each Si/Ge atom shares one valence electron with each of its 4 nearest neighbor atoms,
forming 4 strong covalent bonds in a rigid tetrahedral crystal lattice.
- **UNIT–3:** Semiconductor Devices & Applications and Transistors


### 2.2 Intrinsic (Pure) Semiconductors

- **Definition:** A semiconductor crystal in its purest natural form (99.9999999% pure; fewer than 1
impurity per 1010 atoms).
- **Behavior at Absolute Zero (0 K):** 
Zero thermal energy → all covalent bonds stay intact.
Zero free electrons and zero holes → acts as a perfect insulator.
Behavior at Room Temperature (300 K) — Thermal Generation:
Heat energy vibrates the lattice and breaks a small fraction of covalent bonds.
Each broken bond releases one free electron (e−) into the conduction band.
The missing electron leaves an empty vacancy in the covalent bond called a Hole (h+).
Together, one free electron and one hole created from a broken bond form an Electron–Hole
Pair (EHP).
How a Hole Moves (From-Scratch Concept):
A hole is not a physical particle; it is the absence of a negatively charged electron in a bond.
A neighboring bound valence electron jumps into the empty hole, filling it and leaving a new
hole at its previous site.
Thus, as bound electrons hop left, the hole effectively moves right — behaving exactly like a
mobile positive charge (+q) inside the valence band.
Recombination & Thermal Equilibrium:
Periodically, a free electron loses energy and falls into an empty hole, restoring a covalent bond
(Recombination).
- **At any fixed temperature:** Rate of Thermal Generation = Rate of Recombination.
Carrier Concentration & Fermi Level:
Since electrons and holes are always generated in equal pairs: n = p = ni (where ni is the
intrinsic carrier concentration; for Si at 300 K, ni ≈ 1.5 × 1010 cm−3).
The Fermi level (EFi) sits right at the exact center of the forbidden energy gap.
- **Current Conduction:** Under a battery voltage, electrons drift toward + and holes drift toward −;
both contribute conventional current in the same direction: I = In + Ip.
- **Practical Limitation:** Intrinsic conductivity is very low and uncontrollable because it depends
strictly on temperature — hence doping is required.

### 2.3 Extrinsic (Doped) Semiconductors

What is Doping? The intentional, controlled addition of trace impurity atoms (dopants) into a pure
semiconductor crystal — typically 1 dopant atom per 106 to 108 host atoms.
- **Purpose of Doping:** Increases electrical conductivity by thousands of times and lets engineers
choose whether electrons (N-type) or holes (P-type) dominate conduction.

A. N-Type Semiconductor (Negative-Type / Donor-Doped)
- **Impurity Added:** Pentavalent (Group-V) elements having 5 valence electrons — Phosphorus
(P), Arsenic (As), or Antimony (Sb).
- **Step-by-Step Crystal Mechanism:** 

### 1. One pentavalent atom replaces one Si/Ge atom in the crystal lattice.

2. 4 of its 5 valence electrons form 4 covalent bonds with neighboring Si/Ge atoms.

### 3. The 5th electron is extra and very loosely bound — it needs only 0.05 eV (Si) or 0.01 eV (Ge)

to detach into the conduction band.

### 4. At room temperature (300 K), virtually 100% of pentavalent atoms donate their 5th electron →

hence called Donor Impurities.
- **Immobile Ions Created:** Losing 1 negative electron turns each donor atom into a fixed, immobile
positive donor ion (ND+) locked in the crystal.
- **Charge Carriers:** 
- **Majority Carriers:** Free electrons (n ≈ ND).
- **Minority Carriers:** Thermally generated holes (p = ni2 / ND), so n >> p.
- **Energy Band Effect:** Creates a discrete Donor Energy Level (ED) just 0.05 eV below EC; shifts
the Fermi level (EFn) upward close to the conduction band.
- **Electrical Neutrality Check:** An N-type semiconductor is electrically neutral (NOT negatively
charged!) because every donated mobile electron (−q) is balanced by an immobile positive donor
ion (+q).
B. P-Type Semiconductor (Positive-Type / Acceptor-Doped)
- **Impurity Added:** Trivalent (Group-III) elements having 3 valence electrons — Boron (B),
Gallium (Ga), Indium (In), or Aluminum (Al).
- **Step-by-Step Crystal Mechanism:** 

### 1. One trivalent atom replaces one Si/Ge atom in the lattice.


### 2. Its 3 valence electrons complete 3 covalent bonds with neighboring Si/Ge atoms.


### 3. The 4th covalent bond is left short of 1 electron — forming a built-in hole.


### 4. A nearby valence electron requires only 0.05 eV (Si) to jump into this vacant bond → hence

called Acceptor Impurities.
- **Immobile Ions Created:** Accepting 1 electron turns each trivalent atom into a fixed, immobile
negative acceptor ion (NA−).
- **Charge Carriers:** 
- **Majority Carriers:** Holes (p ≈ NA).
- **Minority Carriers:** Thermally generated free electrons (n = ni2 / NA), so p >> n.
