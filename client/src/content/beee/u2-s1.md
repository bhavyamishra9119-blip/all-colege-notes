## PART I: MAGNETIC MATERIALS, B–H CURVES AND TRANSFORMERS


### 1. Magnetic Materials


#### 1.1 Starting From Scratch: Essential Magnetic Quantities

**Magnetic Flux (Φ):** Total number of magnetic field lines passing through a cross-sectional area —
**SI Unit:** Weber (Wb).
**Magnetic Flux Density (B):** Magnetic flux per unit perpendicular area: B = Φ / A — SI Unit: Tesla
(T) or Wb/m2.
**Magnetomotive Force (MMF):** The magnetic "pressure" produced by current I flowing through an
**N-turn coil:** MMF = N × I — Unit: Ampere-Turns (AT).
**Magnetic Field Intensity (H):** MMF applied per unit length l of the magnetic path: H = (N I) / l —
**Unit:** A/m (AT/m).
**Permeability (μ):** Measures how easily a material allows magnetic flux lines to pass through it:
B = μ H = μ0 μr H, where free-space permeability μ0 = 4π × 10−7 H/m and μr = Relative
Permeability.
**Reluctance (S):** Magnetic opposition to flux (analogous to Resistance in electric circuits): S = l /
(μ0 μr A) = MMF / Φ (AT/Wb).

#### 1.2 Classification of Magnetic Materials (Based on μr)


### 1. Diamagnetic Materials (μr < 1, slightly less than 1)

Weakly repelled by a magnetic field; magnetic flux lines slightly bend away from the material.
Have no permanent atomic magnetic dipoles (paired electron spins cancel out).
**Examples:** Bismuth, Copper, Silver, Gold, Water, Glass.

### 2. Paramagnetic Materials (μr > 1, slightly greater than 1)

Weakly attracted by a magnetic field; atomic dipoles align slightly with an applied field and lose
magnetism instantly once the field is removed.
**Examples:** Aluminum, Platinum, Oxygen, Magnesium, Tungsten.

### 3. Ferromagnetic Materials (μr >> 1, typically 103 to 105)

Strongly attracted by magnets; concentrate thousands of times more magnetic flux than air —
the backbone of all transformers and electric motors!
**Magnetic Domains:** Inside a ferromagnetic crystal, atoms group into microscopic aligned pockets
called Domains. Without an external field, domains point randomly (net B = 0); applying H aligns
all domains in one direction.
**Curie Temperature (Tc):** Above a critical temperature (e.g., 770 °C for iron), thermal agitation
destroys domain alignment and the material becomes paramagnetic.
**Two Engineering Sub-Classes:** 
**Soft Magnetic Materials:** Easy to magnetize and demagnetize; high μr, narrow hysteresis
loop, very low core loss → used in Transformer Cores & Motor Stators/Rotors (e.g., CRGO
Silicon Steel with 3–4% Si, Soft Iron, Ferrites).
**Hard Magnetic Materials:** Difficult to magnetize and demagnetize; very wide hysteresis loop,
high retentivity & coercivity → used for Permanent Magnets (e.g., Alnico, Neodymium NdFeB,
Hard Steel).

### 2. B–H Characteristics (Magnetization Curve & Hysteresis Loop)


#### 2.1 The Initial B–H Magnetization Curve

**Definition:** A graph plotting resulting magnetic flux density B (Y-axis) against applied magnetizing
field intensity H (X-axis) for an initially unmagnetized ferromagnetic core.

**Three Distinct Regions of the B–H Curve:**

1. Linear / High-Permeability Region (O → P): As H rises, magnetic domains align rapidly and B
increases steeply and almost linearly.
2. **Knee Region (Around Point P):** Most domains are already aligned; the slope flattens
noticeably. Transformers are engineered to operate right near the knee point for maximum flux
without excessive current.
3. **Magnetic Saturation Region (P → A):** Nearly 100% of domains are aligned; pushing H much
higher produces almost zero extra rise in B (the core behaves like air!).

#### 2.2 Magnetic Hysteresis & The Full B–H Loop

What Is Hysteresis? From the Greek word meaning "to lag behind" — when an AC magnetizing
force H reverses cycle after cycle, the internal flux density B always lags behind H due to
domain-wall friction.

**Tracing One Complete AC Cycle (6 Key Points):**

1. **O → A (Forward Saturation):** Increasing +H drives the core up to maximum flux density
+Bmax.
2. A → B (Retentivity / Residual Magnetism Br): Reducing H back to 0 does NOT drop B to
zero; the leftover magnetism OB = +Br is called Retentivity (Remanence).
3. **B → C (Coercivity Hc):** Reversing H to a negative value OC = −Hc is required to wipe out
residual magnetism (B = 0); this demagnetizing force is called Coercivity.
4. **C → D (Reverse Saturation):** Increasing negative −H magnetizes the core fully in the opposite
direction (−Bmax).
